# How the Pentagon chose a language

In the mid-seventies the US Department of Defense had a problem that today we'd call a technology zoo. Embedded software for aircraft, ships and command systems was written in hundreds of languages and dialects, every contractor brought its own, and maintaining the whole menagerie cost billions. In January 1975 the High Order Language Working Group, HOLWG, was formed, and its chairman, Air Force Lieutenant Colonel William Whitaker, proposed something unusual for a military agency: first agree, in public, on what the language must do, and only then go looking for the language.

The requirements took several years to write, and each revision got its own name as it hardened: Strawman in April 1975, then Woodenman, Tinman, Ironman and finally Steelman in June 1978. Tinman went out to more than a thousand people around the world, and Dijkstra, Hoare and Wirth all offered advice. Twenty-three existing languages, from Algol 68 to PL/I and Pascal, were honestly checked against it, and the conclusion was that none of them fit and that a new one should be built on the basis of Pascal, PL/I or Algol 68. One amusing detail from Whitaker's recollections: Bell Labs asked that C not be included in the evaluation, and the group honored the request.

In the summer of 1977 the competition was announced. Fourteen proposals came in, and four teams were funded. So that reviewers would judge on substance rather than brand names, the designs were anonymized by color: Green went to CII-Honeywell-Bull, Red to Intermetrics, Blue to SofTech and Yellow to SRI International. According to Whitaker, the colors meant nothing beyond that. Funnily enough, all four teams independently started from Pascal.

In February 1978 the four preliminary designs went out for evaluation, and some eighty teams from military and civilian organizations pored over them. Dijkstra spent the whole of March on them and wrote a review of each. He called Yellow a hopeless mess, faulted Blue for a 160-page chapter on types, ended his review of Green with a phrase about "technical incompetence, probably enhanced by dishonesty," and confessed to being baffled by Red: the design was at once advanced and backward, and he couldn't see how the two went together. He advised Whitaker not to continue with any of the four. The advice wasn't taken: in April 1978 Green and Red went through to the second round.

The second round lasted a year. And here is what matters most for this story. The Red team threw out its first design entirely. The chief designer, John Nestor, rewrote the language from scratch, so there are actually two "red" languages. The first, REDL, was built on monitors and events. The second, RED, the one that reached the final, rested on mailboxes and critical sections. Red's consultants included Barbara Liskov, William Wulf, Paul Abrahams and Robert Dewar, the future author of GNAT. On March 15, 1979, both teams handed in a complete specification, a rationale for their decisions, solutions to the same set of benchmark problems, and a test translator.

Red's translator worked. It was built by Mark Davis's group, and in the recollections of Mary Van Deusen, co-author of the RED Reference Manual, it comes across as a small feat: a compiler for a language that kept changing under it, finished on time. But the second-round contract stated that the translator would not be considered in the selection. Whitaker, for his part, says the same thing from his side: both translators were too incomplete and too slow to decide anything.

On May 2, 1979, the group chose Green. That same day Whitaker sent out three thousand green postcards reading "Green is Ada!" The vote was unanimous, like every vote this group ever took: Whitaker admits he only put a question to a formal vote once everyone already agreed. Van Deusen tells a story from the losing side about a young Air Force representative who had been ordered to vote for Green, discuss nothing and not change his mind. I found no confirmation of this, so let it stand as one side's account.

Why Green won is written, in the documents available, mostly between the lines. The reviewers' decisions exist only on microfiche. Tucker Taft, who joined Intermetrics in 1980 and later led the design of Ada 95, says Green reached stability and completeness earlier than the others, and that Ichbiah was remarkably good at presenting it to the reviewers. Van Deusen recalls that the Green team had a very fine sense of politics from day one, and that its first design decision was the length of the book. And a popular Time-Life book on programming languages from 1986 has what I think is the most accurate line of all: in the final, the two languages swapped places, with Red growing bolder and Green more conservative.

Then came a long polishing period. The Preliminary Ada Reference Manual was published in June 1979 in SIGPLAN Notices; it was picked apart and reworked, and only in February 1983 did the ANSI/MIL-STD-1815A standard appear, the one we now call Ada 83. The standard's number comes from the year Ada Lovelace was born. Intermetrics buried the red language quickly and, according to Van Deusen, completely. But this story has a sequel that I'll come back to: in 1988 the competition to revise Ada was won by an Intermetrics team, so Ada 95 was designed by people from the losers' company, and Ben Brosgol, who had led the Red project, worked with them as a consultant.
