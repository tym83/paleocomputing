# Ada 83, and what came back later

The winning Ada didn't become the Ada everyone knows right away. Four years of revisions separate the green language of spring 1979 from the 1983 standard, and I compare both of them with the red language.

To keep the comparison from being just talk, I took three situations from the RED labs and wrote them in Ada 83, and the project's CI builds these programs with the GNAT compiler in Ada 83 mode and checks their output.

The first situation is a bounded buffer between a producer and a consumer. In RED it's one line, `VAR m : MAILBOX[INT](3)`, plus calls to `SEND` and `RECEIVE`. Ada 83 has no mailboxes, so the buffer is a server task with two entries that, in a loop, picks which entry it can accept right now: `PUT` while there's room, and `GET` while there's something to hand out. It comes to about thirty lines, which anyone who has written Ada has written hundreds of times.

The second is waiting for a reply with a timeout on the caller's side. In RED it's a `WAIT` with two branches. Ada 83 has a timed entry call for this, `select ... or delay ... end select`. But the green language of 1979 didn't have one at all, neither the timed call nor the conditional call. The Green Rationale explains this as a matter of principle: a task can be in only one queue at a time, so a caller doesn't choose among several waits. Four years later Ada partly abandoned that principle.

The third is a critical section. In RED it's `REGION`, which releases the lock however the body ends. In Ada 83 a lock is a task with entries `SEIZE` and `RELEASE`, and if an exception occurs between them, nobody will ever release the lock. You have to remember to do it in the handler, and the line `LOCK.RELEASE; -- forget this line and the next SEIZE waits forever` in my program is about exactly that.

## What changed between the green language and Ada 83

A detailed table of differences is in the project notes; here are the most noticeable ones:

- the green language has no task types, only task families, generic tasks and tasks declared inside a procedure, and tasks are started by an explicit `initiate` statement; moreover, a task that has finished can be started again;
- `select` has no `terminate` alternative, and if all the alternatives are closed and there's no `else`, the `SELECT_ERROR` exception is raised;
- there are fifteen predefined exceptions instead of Ada 83's five, and they're far more specific: `RANGE_ERROR`, `INDEX_ERROR`, `ACCESS_ERROR`, `NO_VALUE_ERROR` for reading an uninitialized variable, and so on;
- visibility is controlled by a `restricted(...)` prefix that can be placed in front of any module, not only a compilation unit; it's the ancestor of Ada 83's `with`;
- the parameter mode is written right at the call site: `F := x`, `F =: v` and `F :=: v`;
- functions aren't allowed to have side effects and can't create objects on the heap; for generators there are value-returning procedures;
- a character literal is a string of length one, `"A"`, and `mod` takes the sign of the left operand, which means it behaves like `rem` in Ada 83;
- there's an `assert` statement.

## What Ada won back later

And now the chapter that I think made the whole article worth writing. Put RED from 1979 next to the history of Ada up to the present day, and you get a strange picture: for decades Ada slowly picked up, piece by piece, what the loser had from the very beginning.

Both finalists had assertions; Steelman required them. Ada 83 dropped the `assert` statement; the word "assert" doesn't appear anywhere in the 1983 standard. The `Assert` pragma came back only in Ada 2005, twenty-six years later.

The rendezvous proved too heavy for protecting shared data. The Ada 95 Rationale says so in as many words: the very generality of the rendezvous means relatively high overhead, so protected objects are introduced, a lightweight, data-oriented synchronization mechanism. In essence that's the very same separate primitive for shared data that RED proposed. An important caveat: the Ada 95 Rationale names Hoare's conditional critical regions, monitors and the Orca language as the ancestors of protected objects, and doesn't mention RED once. So this isn't borrowing but convergence. Telling convergence, though.

It goes further. The Ravenscar profile for high-integrity systems bans task entries outright, `Max_Task_Entries => 0`, which means the most safety-critical flavor of Ada simply has no rendezvous. Ada 2012 added bounded synchronized queues to the standard library, which functionally are a typed, bounded mailbox.

Another line is even more striking. Chapter thirteen of the RED Reference Manual describes user definitions for the language's special forms: your own assignment, automatic initialization and finalization, your own component selection and indexing operations, your own loop over a user-defined type, and your own literals. User-defined assignment and finalization came to Ada in 1995, user-defined indexing and iterators in 2012, user-defined literals in 2022. All that's missing is a user-defined `case`.

And one last thing that genuinely surprised me. In 1988 a competition to revise Ada was announced, and it was won by an Intermetrics team, beating, among others, a team that included Ichbiah himself. The technical lead was Tucker Taft, and the consultants included Ben Brosgol, who had led the Red project in 1979. Ichbiah took part as a reviewer and eventually left the project, disagreeing with the object model. So Ada 95 was designed by people from the company that lost in 1979. Taft himself joined Intermetrics in September 1980 and was never on the Red team, so there's no direct line of descent here. But the company, the culture and some of the people are the same.
