# Two Adas: how I resurrected the language that lost to Ada in 1979 and wrote a compiler, an OS and Kubernetes in it

On May 2, 1979, a small working group at the US Department of Defense voted for a language that a few days later was given the name Ada. The competition had run for four years and seen some fifteen designs pass through it, but only two made it to the final, and right up until the announcement they were known by colors: Green and Red. Green won. It was built by Jean Ichbiah's French-American team. Red, the work of the Boston company Intermetrics, lost and vanished almost without a trace. And yet Red had a working compiler, one that under the terms of the contract nobody was allowed to take into account, and one that nobody has seen since.

I had long wanted to know what exactly lost that day. Not from secondhand accounts, but with my own hands: take the 1979 documents, rebuild both languages from them, write each one's own compiler in that language, and see what these languages can do when you write real programs in them. In September and October 2026 I did just that. Along the way the red language grew a small operating system in the spirit of Tandem NonStop and a Kubernetes control loop. In one of the variants the Kubernetes control plane runs as a Tandem process pair, with no etcd and no quorum, and survives the death of the API server in the middle of a rollout.

This is the third part of my series on paleocomputing. In [the first](https://habr.com/ru/articles/1087928/) I ran Niklaus Wirth's processor in a browser, in QEMU and in Kubernetes; in the second I built Kubernetes on Oberon machines that talk to each other over radio. You don't need to read them first; this story stands on its own.

I've explained why I do all this before, but I'll say it again, because in stories like this the question always comes first, and usually with a smirk. For me this isn't retro for retro's sake, and it isn't about showing off a pretty old thing. I'm interested in the ideas that lived inside technologies that lost: what we threw away along with them, and which of the things we threw away came back later under other names. Ada is especially good material for that. Its requirements were written over years, in public, by the whole world; the finalists were evaluated by hundreds of specialists; and the winner then spent thirty years slowly and expensively winning back what the loser had from the very beginning. There's a whole chapter on that, and to me it's the most instructive one.

The article came out long. First I'll briefly tell how the competition came about and why the Green team won, then what was left of the losers and what I managed to find. Next comes a tour of the red language, called RED, and of the green one as it stood in the spring of 1979, before the rework that turned it into Ada 83. Then how I built compilers that compile themselves, and what the 1979 languages did to me while I was writing in them. After that: the operating system, Kubernetes, a comparison with classic Ada, labs you can work through yourself, and at the end, as I promised myself, an alternative history: what the industry might look like if different hands had gone up in May 1979.

I'll say up front that almost all of the code in the project was written not by me but by AI agents, just as in the earlier parts of the series. My part was the idea, the decisions, the checking, and an endless stream of questions along the lines of "are you sure that's what the Reference Manual says?" Some of the work ran in parallel: one agent reconstructed the grammar of the red language from images of syntax diagrams, another went through the green documents, a third ported the competition's benchmark problems, a fourth dug into the history, and yet another, while I was busy with the red language, built the whole toolchain for the green one. Wherever "reviewers" and "agents" show up in the text, that's who they are.

All the sources, notes and instructions are in the repository [github.com/tym83/paleocomputing](https://github.com/tym83/paleocomputing), under `impl/ada-colors`.
