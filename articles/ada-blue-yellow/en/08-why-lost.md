# Why they lost

The Pittsburgh panel that pulled the analyses together consisted of David Fisher, David Gries, Brian Kernighan, John Reynolds, Philip Wetherall and William Wulf. Its report is dated April 4, 1978. The panel opens by saying that "all four designs are incomplete and show signs of haste", and its conclusion turned out simple. The reviewers considered Green the best and Blue the worst, and saw almost no difference between Red and Yellow. If you read the analyses themselves, though, the picture is far more varied. Some teams put Yellow first, others last. One wrote that Yellow was the worst-designed of all and shouldn't be continued; another considered it a reliable choice for the systems programmer and, apparently, the cheapest to implement. And yet another ended its analysis with my favourite piece of advice: the best thing the DoD could do would be to ask the red team to implement the yellow language. Well, the yellow language has now been implemented.

![Summary of the panel's recommendations of April 4, 1978: continue Green, reject Blue, continue at least one of Red and Yellow](../img/scan-pittsburgh.png)

On Blue the panel was the most brief: "Reject Blue." The design "is careful and complete", but "it is overwhelmed by a pervasive complexity; the 'simple underlying semantic model' claimed by the designers is not evident". One team admitted that Blue had suffered from its own presentation and that this had surely affected its assessment, and in its summary table Blue and Yellow got the lowest mark for how easily a typo changes the meaning of a program. The rebuild confirmed both halves of the verdict. The care is real: the input/output library with its drivers started working after a dozen typos and seven breaches of the language rules were fixed, and the two kinds of semaphores turned out more precise than anything the finalists had. But the complexity is real too: Blue's translator came out the biggest of the four, and the backslashes, hash signs and double exclamation marks read worse than in any of its neighbours.

Yellow lost by a hair. The panel wrote that "the choice between Red and Yellow is not clear cut", and "although the margin is very thin, the majority of the panel believes that Red is slightly to be preferred". But in the section on Yellow itself the words are harsh: type and representation are confused, "there is no workable mechanism for process identification", "monitors have not been used for significant systems", and on the whole "the attempt to build on Pascal and its cousin Modula has not been successful". Here my experience agrees with the panel by half. On process identification it's right exactly where I stumbled myself. But monitors turned out to be the most convenient part of the yellow language for THE and Kubernetes, and by 1978 Brinch Hansen had already, two years earlier, described the Solo operating system, written with Concurrent Pascal monitors. The irony is also that the red design, which beat Yellow so narrowly, was soon thrown out by the red team itself, which wrote the language anew in the second round.

Put the four languages side by side and you see four answers to one question: how many synchronization mechanisms does a language need. Green chose the rendezvous, Blue semaphores, Yellow monitors, and only Red offered several mechanisms at once. Three designs out of four bet on a single main mechanism, and in none of those three can a caller simply say "I'll wait no longer than so many milliseconds": in Green only the server has a timeout, in Blue you're left with a race of paths, in Yellow with a watchdog or polling. And in the chapter on Ada we'll see that Ada eventually took the monitor back too, under the name of the protected object. The book's compilers wrote that the losing designs "should be of interest only to researchers". It seems they were right, except that the researchers showed up forty-eight years later.
